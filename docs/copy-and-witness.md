# Copy, restore and the witness

[← README](../README.md)

## Copy and restore

With `replica.url` set (`s3://bucket/prefix?endpoint=…&region=…` or `file:///path`), the evidence log and the chain are copied in immutable chunks, together with signed chain heads. Nothing is ever deleted from the copy, and keys never go there. The chunks are encrypted (AES-256-GCM) with `copy.key`, 32 random bytes that `init` puts into `data_dir` and that must reach `keys.backup_dir` before anything is copied: while it is not confirmed there, nothing is copied (`evidencestore_copy_key_backup_pending`). Each chunk is bound to its name in the copy, so a chunk moved under another name, offset or generation does not open. The signed heads stay readable: they are what the copy is checked against and what `restore` picks a generation by. Whoever holds the copy sees chunk sizes, when they arrive, the names of the files they belong to (a name carries the number of the file's first record or leaf) and the heads — how fast the store grows — but no source names, records or receive times; with `copy.key` they would see those, and the bodies stay under the month keys either way. `replica.url` therefore needs `keys.backup_dir`: a copy whose key is lost with `data_dir` cannot be restored. A store that finds chunks it cannot open under `replica.url` (another installation wrote them, or `copy.key` is not this store's) does not write there. S3 credentials for the copy come from `AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID` and `AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY` (with `AWS_SESSION_TOKEN` if set); the region is `region=` in the URL, otherwise `AWS_REGION`, otherwise `us-east-1`. The directory of a `file://` URL is not created by the store, since a missing one looks like an unmounted NFS or rclone mount: create it on the mounted disk, as a directory inside it rather than the mount point itself. Until it is there, every pass fails (`evidencestore_replica_errors_total`) and nothing lands on the local disk. `evidencestore restore` opens the chunks with `copy.key` from `keys.backup_dir` (or `-copy-key FILE`), puts that key into the new `data_dir`, rebuilds the log and the chain from the copy and checks the chain against the latest signed head whose link the copy holds: the root of the tree over its links up to that head must be the head's root. The chain is cut at that head: a pass that was interrupted before its head may have left links whose leaves never arrived, or leaves whose links never did, and nothing the head does not sign is taken; records past the head are linked again from the evidence log when the store starts. The restored store does not write on the copy in place, since its bytes would land on that cut tail: it writes the copy again as a new generation, from scratch, under `gen/<n>/` in the same location. Earlier generations stay as they are, and `restore` takes the newest generation that holds a signed head. On its first pass after start the store also checks that the copy ends where `data_dir` does. If `data_dir` lost data that had already been copied (a disk that lied about fsync), or did not write that copy, the store refuses to write on it (`the copy is not where data_dir left it` in the log, `evidencestore_replica_errors_total` grows): restore from the copy into an empty `data_dir`, or point `replica.url` at a new location. The evidence log and the leaves are not checked against the chain at that point: a chunk damaged in the copy does not decrypt and stops `restore`, but a file already damaged in `data_dir` before it was copied shows only at the first start, which checks every record already in the chain and refuses one that differs (records past the last signed head have no leaf to check against), so do not discard another copy of `data_dir` on the strength of `restore` alone. `keyring.dat` and `sign.key` come back from `keys.backup_dir` (the store checks `sign.key` against that copy on every start, and refuses to start with a chain but without `sign.key`; so for `copy.key`, which `restore` puts in place itself — put it back by hand when `data_dir` is rebuilt without `restore`), the roots from `keys.roots_mirror_dir`. On start the store checks that every record in the log is sealed with a key that `keyring.dat` knows: a wrong or too old `keyring.dat` stops the start instead of leaving bodies unreadable. What the store accepted after the last copy is lost with the disk: collectors keep the signed promises as proof, and the next record or heartbeat of each source shows the gap in the chain. So is a collector binding made after the last copy: that source is bound again to the first installation that contacts the restored store, and a legitimate one refused meanwhile is accepted with `sources bind`. A record whose evidence reached the copy but whose chain link did not is linked when the restored store starts, in links no longer than a chain tick by receive time, as the lost ones were, each carrying the receive time of the last record it links: the promise check reads time-stamps, not link times, and no time-stamp is taken before they are linked, so it does not show that delay; for a Pipelock source, the entry after a lost one is marked as not intact (its chain link to the previous entry is broken).

## Witness of the chain heads

Whoever holds `sign.key` can sign a second version of the chain, and a bundle from it verifies on its own. Only someone who saw a head early and keeps it out of that administrator's reach can show that the chain was rewritten: a witness. A head is a checkpoint in the [C2SP format](https://c2sp.org/tlog-checkpoint): the number of links and the root of a Merkle tree (RFC 6962) over all of them, signed with `sign.key` (the signature is also a valid C2SP signed note with the origin as the key name), so a head the witness holds fixes the whole chain up to it, and whether one head extends another is shown by a few hashes. TSA time-stamps certify the same number and root.

With `witness.url` set (`s3://bucket/prefix?endpoint=…&region=…` or `file:///path`), the store sends the witness its signed chain heads only — origin (`evidencestore/` and 16 hex digits of the key's hash), number of links, root and signature; no records and no source names, only how fast the chain grows — under `heads/<number>.json` (the number zero-padded to 20 digits): the head under every TSA time-stamp, where a single-record bundle ends, the head of the link that holds its token, where window and one-source bundles end, and the current head every `witness.every` (1 minute by default). With a copy configured, the witness never gets ahead of it: only heads the copy already holds after a successful pass are sent, so a restore from the copy cannot contradict the witness, and heads reach it up to `replica.every` later. S3 credentials come only from `ES_WITNESS_ACCESS_KEY_ID` and `ES_WITNESS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY` (and `ES_WITNESS_SESSION_TOKEN`), never from the copy's; the region is found as for the copy. `witness.url` must differ from `replica.url`. As with the copy, the directory of a `file://` witness is not created: while it is missing, every send fails (`evidencestore_witness_errors_total`).

The witness has to be run by someone the store's administrator does not control: the auditor, another organization, or a cloud bucket with S3 Object Lock in compliance mode, which not even the account owner can delete from before the retention ends. S3 on a host where that administrator has root is a backup, not a witness: root deletes or rewrites its files past S3. Give the store a user that may only write objects, and keep versioning on: an overwrite then adds a version, and the original one stays. Tried on MinIO with Object Lock: a locked version cannot be deleted, but a newer version or a delete marker hides it from a plain read and from the listing, so take all versions.

An auditor checks a bundle against the witness with `esverify -heads DIR`, where `DIR` holds the heads they took from the witness themselves, all versions (for S3, `aws s3api list-object-versions` with `get-object --version-id`, or `rclone copy --s3-versions`; any S3-compatible storage, through `--endpoint-url`). Files that are not heads signed with the store key are skipped. Of the heads at or after the record's link, the one the witness received first fixes the record: a chain rewritten after the witness saw that head does not agree with it. `esverify` takes the first such head by number instead and does not yet know when the witness received a head: see the next paragraph. Two different heads with one number prove that the store signed two versions of the chain. Where that touches the record's part of the chain, the bundle does not verify; elsewhere the record stays proven, and the report names the second version: a rewrite, or a store that lost data without a copy. If the head `esverify` takes lies past the end of the bundle, ask for a longer one: `evidencestore proof … -to-head N`. Anything else the administrator cannot take back works as a witness too, for example `evidencestore statement` mailed or published once a day: check against those heads the same way, in a `DIR` of their own. With a copy configured, `statement` gives the head the copy holds, as the witness gets, so a restore from the copy does not contradict it.

That leaves a gap: a head signed later and added to the witness under an unused number between the record's link and the head that fixes it comes first by number, and `esverify` takes it instead. Whoever holds both `sign.key` and the witness credentials can add one (P-21 in [`docs/dev/FINDINGS.md`](dev/FINDINGS.md)). The store sends its heads in the order of their numbers, so a head added that way reached the witness after heads with higher numbers, and that gives it away. Until `esverify` checks this, check it yourself, for each witness on its own with a `DIR` of its own; the bundle has to pass against each. `esverify` names the head it took (`head N`, in its line about the witness's heads): no head with a higher number may have reached that witness before the earliest version of `heads/<N>.json` did. If one did, head N was added later and fixes nothing: ask for a bundle up to the first head above N that passes this check (`evidencestore proof … -to-head M`) and verify it again. `esverify` names head N again, but the bundle now reaches M and has to agree with it. S3 gives each version the time it received it (`LastModified` in `list-object-versions`): on AWS the writer cannot choose it, and on other S3-compatible storage check that a user who may only write objects cannot set it. For a statement mailed to you, take the time your mail server received it, not the date in the message; for a published one, its date. In a collector's head journal the time is `received_at`, by the collector's clock. A `file://` witness keeps no such time, since whoever writes a file sets its time: it works only with snapshots you take of it on a schedule, a head then counts as received at your first snapshot that holds it, and a rewrite made before that snapshot is not caught.

## Collectors as witnesses

When the store and the sources are run by different people, the collectors catch a rewritten chain themselves, with no third party. Every answer the store gives a collector, to a batch or a heartbeat, carries the store's signed head — with a copy configured, the head the copy holds, and none until the copy's first pass after the store starts — and a proof of a few hashes that this head extends the one the collector accepted last (the collector names it in each request). The collector checks the signature with `store_key`, that the chain did not get shorter, that a head of the same length has the same root, and the proof. A head that passes becomes its last one. At most every `checkpoint_every` (10 minutes by default, at least a second), and when it stops, the collector writes the last head to `checkpoint.json` in `spool_dir`, which it goes on from after a restart, and then to `checkpoints.jsonl` with the time it writes the line by its own clock; the lines keep the order of receipt. The first head after a collector is installed is taken on trust, as `store_key` is.

A head that does not extend the last one means the store signed a second history or lost the end of its chain: the collector logs an error and writes both heads to `checkpoints-conflict.jsonl` (both are signed by the store, and together they are the evidence), keeps its last head and goes on delivering records, since stopping would put the records at risk. A newer head without the proof gives only a warning and the last head stays: the store sends none while its derived `tree.hashes` is damaged (and, with a copy, no head at all). But when for more than three `checkpoint_every` the store shows no head that extends the last one — answers without a head, without the proof, or with the same head while the collector delivers records (they enter the chain within a tick, or with a copy within `replica.every`) — the collector logs an error: it does not witness the chain meanwhile. Time while requests to the store fail (no connection, an error status) does not count; the time between answers does, however rarely the collector asks. With a copy, heads move on at the pace of its passes: keep the store's `replica.every` (1 minute by default) under three `checkpoint_every`, or the collectors report this error between passes. Errors and warnings repeat at most once per `checkpoint_every` while they last. None of this stops the delivery of records: a head file that cannot be written gives an error in the log, and a line torn by a crash is cut when the collector starts. With a copy configured, the store gives collectors the head the copy holds, as it gives the witness, so an honest restore from the copy does not look like a second history to them. Without a copy, a store that lost the end of its chain (a disk that lied about fsync) does look like one, and rightly: its history did change. To accept the new history (after you have looked into it), stop the collector, move `checkpoint.json`, `checkpoints.jsonl` and the conflict file aside and start it again; keep them as evidence, and do not give auditors the old conflict file together with the new journal: bundles of the new history agree with heads it refused, and do not verify against it. If only `checkpoint.json` is gone, the collector goes on from the last head in its journal. A `checkpoint.json` that cannot be read or does not verify with `store_key` is set aside together with the journal (as `*.unverified-<time>`), and the collector takes the next head as its first.

An auditor uses the collectors' files like a witness: `esverify -heads DIR` reads `checkpoints.jsonl`, `checkpoint.json` and `checkpoints-conflict.jsonl` as they are; a torn line costs only itself, and nothing else in `DIR` (a proof bundle, say) counts as a head. From a conflict line, the head the collector held counts as its head, and the head it refused is evidence, never a head that fixes a record: a bundle that agrees with a refused head does not verify, even when another collector accepted the same head (it may have taken it on trust as its first one), and the report names refused heads. The gap described above for a witness bucket does not arise in a collector's journal: the store cannot put a head there, and the collector writes only heads that extend every head already in it, so a head of a rewritten chain gets there only if the rewrite comes after the last head the collector saved. A store that shows different collectors different histories is caught where a bundle covers heads of both (it agrees with one of them only) or where two of their heads have the same number of links; `esverify` does not compare heads of different lengths with each other. Against an administrator who runs the sources as well, the collectors do not help: they can rewrite the collectors' files too. A collector learns only the heads: how fast the whole store grows, not what it holds.

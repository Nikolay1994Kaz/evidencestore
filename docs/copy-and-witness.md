# Copy, restore and the witness

[← README](../README.md)

## Copy and restore

With `replica.url` set (`s3://bucket/prefix?endpoint=…&region=…` or `file:///path`), the evidence log and the chain are copied in immutable chunks, together with signed chain heads. Nothing is ever deleted from the copy, and keys never go there. The chunks are encrypted (AES-256-GCM) with `copy.key`, 32 random bytes that `init` puts into `data_dir` and that must reach `keys.backup_dir` before anything is copied: while it is not confirmed there, nothing is copied (`evidencestore_copy_key_backup_pending`). Each chunk is bound to its name in the copy, so a chunk moved under another name, offset or generation does not open. The signed heads stay readable: they are what the copy is checked against and what `restore` picks a generation by. Whoever holds the copy sees chunk sizes, when they arrive, the names of the files they belong to (a name carries the number of the file's first record or leaf) and the heads — how fast the store grows — but no source names, records or receive times; with `copy.key` they would see those, and the bodies stay under the month keys either way. `replica.url` therefore needs `keys.backup_dir`: a copy whose key is lost with `data_dir` cannot be restored. A store that finds chunks it cannot open under `replica.url` (another installation wrote them, or `copy.key` is not this store's) does not write there. S3 credentials for the copy come from `AWS_ACCESS_KEY_ID` and `AWS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY` (with `AWS_SESSION_TOKEN` if set); the region is `region=` in the URL, otherwise `AWS_REGION`, otherwise `us-east-1`. The directory of a `file://` URL is not created by the store, since a missing one looks like an unmounted NFS or rclone mount: create it on the mounted disk, as a directory inside it rather than the mount point itself. Until it is there, every pass fails (`evidencestore_replica_errors_total`) and nothing lands on the local disk. `evidencestore restore` opens the chunks with `copy.key` from `keys.backup_dir` (or `-copy-key FILE`), puts that key into the new `data_dir`, rebuilds the log and the chain from the copy and checks the chain against the latest signed head whose link the copy holds: the root of the tree over its links up to that head must be the head's root. The chain is cut at that head: a pass that was interrupted before its head may have left links whose leaves never arrived, or leaves whose links never did, and nothing the head does not sign is taken; records past the head are linked again from the evidence log when the store starts. The restored store does not write on the copy in place, since its bytes would land on that cut tail: it writes the copy again as a new generation, from scratch, under `gen/<n>/` in the same location. Earlier generations stay as they are, and `restore` takes the newest generation that holds a signed head. On its first pass after start the store also checks that the copy ends where `data_dir` does. If `data_dir` lost data that had already been copied (a disk that lied about fsync), or did not write that copy, the store refuses to write on it (`the copy is not where data_dir left it` in the log, `evidencestore_replica_errors_total` grows): restore from the copy into an empty `data_dir`, or point `replica.url` at a new location. The evidence log and the leaves are not checked against the chain at that point: a chunk damaged in the copy does not decrypt and stops `restore`, but a file already damaged in `data_dir` before it was copied shows only at the first start, which checks every record already in the chain and refuses one that differs (records past the last signed head have no leaf to check against), so do not discard another copy of `data_dir` on the strength of `restore` alone. `keyring.dat` and `sign.key` come back from `keys.backup_dir` (the store checks `sign.key` against that copy on every start, and refuses to start with a chain but without `sign.key`; so for `copy.key`, which `restore` puts in place itself — put it back by hand when `data_dir` is rebuilt without `restore`), the roots from `keys.roots_mirror_dir`. On start the store checks that every record in the log is sealed with a key that `keyring.dat` knows: a wrong or too old `keyring.dat` stops the start instead of leaving bodies unreadable. What the store accepted after the last copy is lost with the disk: collectors keep the signed promises as proof, and the next record or heartbeat of each source shows the gap in the chain. So is a collector binding made after the last copy: that source is bound again to the first installation that contacts the restored store, and a legitimate one refused meanwhile is accepted with `sources bind`. A record whose evidence reached the copy but whose chain link did not is linked when the restored store starts, in links no longer than a chain tick by receive time, as the lost ones were, each carrying the receive time of the last record it links: the promise check reads time-stamps, not link times, and no time-stamp is taken before they are linked, so it does not show that delay; for a Pipelock source, the entry after a lost one is marked as not intact (its chain link to the previous entry is broken).

## Witness of the chain heads

Whoever holds `sign.key` can sign a second version of the chain, and a bundle from it verifies on its own. Only someone who saw a head early and keeps it out of that administrator's reach can show that the chain was rewritten: a witness. A head is a checkpoint in the [C2SP format](https://c2sp.org/tlog-checkpoint): the number of links and the root of a Merkle tree (RFC 6962) over all of them, signed with `sign.key` (the signature is also a valid C2SP signed note with the origin as the key name), so a head the witness holds fixes the whole chain up to it, and whether one head extends another is shown by a few hashes. TSA time-stamps certify the same number and root.

With `witness.url` set (`s3://bucket/prefix?endpoint=…&region=…` or `file:///path`), the store sends the witness its signed chain heads only — origin (`evidencestore/` and 16 hex digits of the key's hash), number of links, root and signature; no records and no source names, only how fast the chain grows — under `heads/<number>.json` (the number zero-padded to 20 digits): the head under every TSA time-stamp, where a single-record bundle ends, the head of the link that holds its token, where window and one-source bundles end, and the current head every `witness.every` (1 minute by default). With a copy configured, the witness never gets ahead of it: only heads the copy already holds after a successful pass are sent, so a restore from the copy cannot contradict the witness, and heads reach it up to `replica.every` later. S3 credentials come only from `ES_WITNESS_ACCESS_KEY_ID` and `ES_WITNESS_SECRET_ACCESS_KEY` (and `ES_WITNESS_SESSION_TOKEN`), never from the copy's; the region is found as for the copy. `witness.url` must differ from `replica.url`. As with the copy, the directory of a `file://` witness is not created: while it is missing, every send fails (`evidencestore_witness_errors_total`).

The witness has to be run by someone the store's administrator does not control: the auditor, another organization, or a cloud bucket with S3 Object Lock in compliance mode, which not even the account owner can delete from before the retention ends. S3 on a host where that administrator has root is a backup, not a witness: root deletes or rewrites its files past S3. Give the store a user that may only write objects, and keep versioning on: an overwrite then adds a version, and the original one stays. Tried on MinIO with Object Lock: a locked version cannot be deleted, but a newer version or a delete marker hides it from a plain read and from the listing, so take all versions.

An auditor checks a bundle against the witness with `esverify -heads DIR`, where `DIR` holds the heads they took from the witness themselves, all versions (for S3, every version with `aws s3api get-object --version-id` and the versions list next to them, see below; any S3-compatible storage, through `--endpoint-url`). Files that are not heads signed with the store key are skipped. Of the heads at or after the record's link, the one the witness received first fixes the record: a chain rewritten after the witness saw that head does not agree with it. Two different heads with one number prove that the store signed two versions of the chain. Where that touches the record's part of the chain, the bundle does not verify; elsewhere the record stays proven, and the report names the second version: a rewrite, or a store that lost data without a copy. If the head `esverify` takes lies past the end of the bundle, ask for a longer one: `evidencestore proof … -to-head N`. Anything else the administrator cannot take back works as a witness too, for example `evidencestore statement` mailed or published once a day: check against those heads the same way, in a `DIR` of their own. With a copy configured, `statement` gives the head the copy holds, as the witness gets, so a restore from the copy does not contradict it.

When the witness received a head is what `esverify` goes by, where the directory says it. Whoever holds both `sign.key` and the witness credentials — the store's administrator: the store writes its heads to the witness — can sign a head later and add it under an unused number between the record's link and the head that fixes it; Object Lock does not stop adding. By number that head comes first; it reached the witness later, after heads with higher numbers (P-21 in [`docs/dev/FINDINGS.md`](dev/FINDINGS.md)). So of the heads at or after the record's link `esverify` takes the one the witness received first (on equal times, the higher number), and the bundle has to reach it and agree with it; the report names it with the time. Each witness in `DIR` does this on its own, by its own clock, and the bundle has to reach the head of every one of them: times of different witnesses are never compared. One first head for all of them would let a head the administrator put into the bucket outrank a collector that received the honest head earlier but wrote it to its journal later (it writes at most every `checkpoint_every`), or whose clock is behind (P-94). `esverify` tells witnesses apart like this: an S3 witness is its versions list (one list per bucket, and the files of its versions may lie anywhere in `DIR`); a collector is its files, `checkpoints.jsonl`, `checkpoint.json` and `checkpoints-conflict.jsonl` in one subdirectory or under a common prefix (`A-checkpoints.jsonl`), and a file under another name is a witness of its own; heads without a time are told apart by subdirectory (P-99, P-101). Give each collector, and each witness without times, a subdirectory of its own. A file named after a version ID is that version and nothing else; a file that matches a version only by its bytes, such as a mailed statement of a head the bucket also holds, counts as well as a witness without a time in its subdirectory, so a copy the administrator puts into the bucket later does not swallow it (P-104). A journal the collector set aside at start (`….unverified-<time>`) counts with its other files (P-105). Times come from:

- **S3.** Put the versions list next to the heads, one file per bucket, `aws s3api list-object-versions --bucket B --prefix P/heads/ > DIR/versions.json` (without `--max-items`: a list saved page by page is refused, with that advice, P-105), and every version it names, each to its own file (`aws s3api get-object --bucket B --key K --version-id V DIR/V.json`). S3 gives each version the time it received it (`LastModified`): on AWS the writer cannot choose it, and on other S3-compatible storage check that a user who may only write objects cannot set it. `esverify` finds the version of each head file by its ETag, which is the MD5 of a single-part upload (the store writes each head with one `PUT`), or, on a bucket where the ETag is not an MD5 (SSE-KMS), by the file name, which is then the version ID with `.json` (versions written before versioning was turned on all have the ID `null` and are found by the ETag only); a head stored more than once counts from its earliest version. A head from a file that found its version gets that version's time only: what the file says inside, a `received_at` or a collector's refusal included, was written by whoever put it into the bucket (P-96, P-100). A version whose file is not a head, such as junk put over a head, counts as downloaded and changes nothing. A version in the list that no file is, in the bundle's part of the chain or received no later than the head that fixes the record, fails the check: download it.
- **A collector's head journal.** Its lines carry `received_at`, by the collector's clock.

Without times — no versions list, a mailed `evidencestore statement`, a `file://` witness — `esverify` takes the first head by number, as before, and the report says that the heads carry no time of receipt. Then check it yourself, for each witness on its own with a `DIR` of its own: no head with a higher number may have reached that witness before the head `esverify` names (`head N`, in its line about the witness's heads). If one did, head N was added later and fixes nothing: ask for a bundle up to the first head above N that passes this check (`evidencestore proof … -to-head M`) and verify it again. For a statement mailed to you, take the time your mail server received it, not the date in the message; for a published one, its date. A `file://` witness keeps no such time, since whoever writes a file sets its time: it works only with snapshots you take of it on a schedule, a head then counts as received at your first snapshot that holds it, and a rewrite made before that snapshot is not caught. Whatever the source of times, a head fixes only what was in the chain when the witness received it: a rewrite made before the first head after the record reached the witness — within `witness.every`, a minute by default — is not caught by that witness.

## Cosigning witnesses (C2SP)

A witness bucket holds heads, and when it received each one is kept by the bucket, next to the heads the administrator with the witness credentials can still add (P-21). A cosigning witness answers for itself instead: it cosigns a head only if it extends the last head it cosigned for that store, and its signature carries the time it signed. The store speaks the C2SP protocols for this, [tlog-witness](https://c2sp.org/tlog-witness) and [tlog-cosignature](https://c2sp.org/tlog-cosignature) (Ed25519 cosignatures, `cosignature/v1`), and `eswitness` is such a witness, shipped with evidencestore. It was tested against `eswitness`, the test vectors of the specifications and the Go reference implementation of signed notes, not yet against witnesses of other projects; those need the store's verifier key in their own format, which `evidencestore status` shows as `log_vkey`.

List each witness in `witness.cosigners` with the address it takes heads on (the store posts to `<url>/add-checkpoint` and follows no redirects) and its verifier key as `eswitness vkey` prints it, taken from whoever runs the witness, not from the store host; `ca` names a PEM file with the CAs of the witness's HTTPS certificate when the system ones do not cover it. Up to 8 witnesses, with or without `witness.url`:

```json
"witness": {"cosigners": [{"url": "https://witness.example:8444", "key": "witness.example/w1+<8 hex>+<base64>"}]}
```

Each witness gets the heads `witness.url` gets: the head under every TSA time-stamp, the head of the link that holds its token, and every `witness.every` the current head — with a copy configured, the head the copy holds, so that a restore from the copy never ends the chain before a head the witness cosigned (it would never cosign that store again). With each head goes a consistency proof from the last head the witness cosigned; the witnesses are served in parallel, so one that hangs does not hold up the others. The store checks every cosignature with the configured key before it keeps it in `data_dir/cosign/cosignatures.jsonl` (synced line by line); that file goes to the copy with the evidence log and comes back with `restore`, since a witness keeps only its latest head and cannot give an older cosignature again. Every bundle carries, for each witness, the cosignature of the first head at or after the record's link (for a window bundle, the end of its stretch; for a one-source bundle, the link of the token that closes it). A cosigned head may lie past the end of the bundle; the report then names the head to ask for. `evidencestore status` shows, for each witness, the last head it cosigned, when, and the last error.

After a start the store asks each witness from the last head it holds a cosignature of, not from zero, and learns the witness's head from its answer (`409` with its size). When the witness has cosigned a head whose cosignature the store lost (a crash between the witness's answer and the write), the store asks for that head again. When the witness names a head older than one it has cosigned for this store, it has lost its state, and from there it would cosign a second history under the same key: the store raises `evidencestore_cosigner_lost_state` and does not go on from the witness's older head.

An auditor passes each witness's verifier key, taken from that witness's operator, with `esverify -witness-key VKEY` (or a file holding it; repeat the flag for more witnesses). Every witness named so must have cosigned a head at or after the record, and the chain in the bundle must agree with it; for a cosigned head past the end of the bundle, ask for one up to it (`evidencestore proof … -to-head N`). The report names the head each witness cosigned, the witness's key ID and the time. A cosignature says: this head, with this root, at this time, extends every head I cosigned before. A rewrite of the record's part of the chain after that time contradicts it, and so does every head of a rewritten chain the witness is shown later: whoever holds `sign.key` cannot get a cosignature with an earlier time, nor slip in a head the witness did not check. A rewrite made before the witness cosigned its first head after the record (within `witness.every`, or a copy pass with a copy configured) is not caught, as with any witness. In a single-record bundle, a witness whose clock was behind shows up when it dates a cosignature before T1, the time-stamp the record follows: the report says its time cannot be relied on. Without `-witness-key` the cosignatures are not checked, and the report says how many the bundle carries; an `esverify` older than this release ignores them.

A head that does not extend the last one cosigned is refused (`422`): the witness holds another history of this store's chain, a rewrite or a `data_dir` that lost heads the witness had seen. The store raises `evidencestore_cosigner_refused` and keeps offering that witness the refused head, and no head after it, every `witness.every`. A witness that has cosigned more links than the chain holds (a `data_dir` restored from an older copy, or another history) gets no head either, and the store says so in its log and in `status`. `eswitness` keeps in its `refused.jsonl` the evidence of a second history: a head of the same size as one it cosigned and with another root, both signed by the store, once per such pair while it runs. A consistency proof that does not verify is only logged, at most once a minute: the sender supplies the proof, and anyone holding a signed head can send a wrong one.

A witness never forgets. Do not move its state aside or bring its `data_dir` back from an older backup: a witness that forgot a head it cosigned would cosign a second history under the same key, the very thing it exists to catch, and auditors could not tell. `eswitness` keeps every head it cosigned in `data_dir/cosigned.jsonl` too, and refuses to start when a store's state is missing or older than that journal. To go on after the store's history changed, or after the witness lost its `data_dir`, start a new witness with a new key (`eswitness init` with a new `data_dir`), put its verifier key into `witness.cosigners` and give it to auditors; cosignatures of the old key stay valid for the history they cover.

`eswitness` takes a config ([`configs/eswitness.example.json`](../configs/eswitness.example.json)): `name`, which goes into every cosignature and the verifier key (a URL without the scheme under its operator's control, such as `witness.example/w1`), `listen`, `data_dir`, optionally `tls` with `cert` and `key` (without it, plain HTTP, for a TLS proxy in front: the cosignature does not depend on the transport), and in `logs` the stores it cosigns, each by `store_key` as `evidencestore init` prints it. `eswitness init` creates `data_dir/witness.key` and prints the verifier key, `eswitness vkey` prints it again, and `eswitness serve` takes heads at `POST /add-checkpoint` and gives the last head it cosigned for a store, with the store's signature and its own, at `GET /<sha256 of the store's origin, lowercase hex>/checkpoint`. It keeps one file per store under `data_dir/state/`, written to disk before it answers, and a damaged one stops the start. A cosignature carries the witness's clock; when that clock steps back a little (NTP), cosignatures keep the time of the last one, and when it is set right after running far ahead, they take the current time again. Run it under someone the store's administrator does not control, like any witness: whoever controls its host or its key can cosign any history.

## Collectors as witnesses

When the store and the sources are run by different people, the collectors catch a rewritten chain themselves, with no third party. Every answer the store gives a collector, to a batch or a heartbeat, carries the store's signed head — with a copy configured, the head the copy holds, and none until the copy's first pass after the store starts — and a proof of a few hashes that this head extends the one the collector accepted last (the collector names it in each request). The collector checks the signature with `store_key`, that the chain did not get shorter, that a head of the same length has the same root, and the proof. A head that passes becomes its last one. At most every `checkpoint_every` (10 minutes by default, at least a second), and when it stops, the collector writes the last head to `checkpoint.json` in `spool_dir`, which it goes on from after a restart, and then to `checkpoints.jsonl` with the time it writes the line by its own clock; the lines keep the order of receipt. The first head after a collector is installed is taken on trust, as `store_key` is.

A head that does not extend the last one means the store signed a second history or lost the end of its chain: the collector logs an error and writes both heads to `checkpoints-conflict.jsonl` (both are signed by the store, and together they are the evidence), keeps its last head and goes on delivering records, since stopping would put the records at risk. A newer head without the proof gives only a warning and the last head stays: the store sends none while its derived `tree.hashes` is damaged (and, with a copy, no head at all). But when for more than three `checkpoint_every` the store shows no head that extends the last one — answers without a head, without the proof, or with the same head while the collector delivers records (they enter the chain within a tick, or with a copy within `replica.every`) — the collector logs an error: it does not witness the chain meanwhile. Time while requests to the store fail (no connection, an error status) does not count; the time between answers does, however rarely the collector asks. With a copy, heads move on at the pace of its passes: keep the store's `replica.every` (1 minute by default) under three `checkpoint_every`, or the collectors report this error between passes. Errors and warnings repeat at most once per `checkpoint_every` while they last. None of this stops the delivery of records: a head file that cannot be written gives an error in the log, and a line torn by a crash is cut when the collector starts. With a copy configured, the store gives collectors the head the copy holds, as it gives the witness, so an honest restore from the copy does not look like a second history to them. Without a copy, a store that lost the end of its chain (a disk that lied about fsync) does look like one, and rightly: its history did change. To accept the new history (after you have looked into it), stop the collector, move `checkpoint.json`, `checkpoints.jsonl` and the conflict file aside and start it again; keep them as evidence, and do not give auditors the old conflict file together with the new journal: bundles of the new history agree with heads it refused, and do not verify against it. If only `checkpoint.json` is gone, the collector goes on from the last head in its journal. A `checkpoint.json` that cannot be read or does not verify with `store_key` is set aside together with the journal (as `*.unverified-<time>`), and the collector takes the next head as its first.

An auditor uses the collectors' files like a witness, each collector's files in a subdirectory of `DIR` of their own: `esverify -heads DIR` reads `checkpoints.jsonl`, `checkpoint.json` and `checkpoints-conflict.jsonl` as they are, and each subdirectory fixes the record on its own; a torn line costs only itself, and nothing else in `DIR` (a proof bundle, say) counts as a head. From a conflict line, the head the collector held counts as its head, and the head it refused is evidence, never a head that fixes a record: a bundle that agrees with a refused head does not verify, even when another collector accepted the same head (it may have taken it on trust as its first one), and the report names refused heads. The gap described above for a witness bucket does not arise in a collector's journal: the store cannot put a head there, and the collector writes only heads that extend every head already in it, so a head of a rewritten chain gets there only if the rewrite comes after the last head the collector saved. A store that shows different collectors different histories is caught where a bundle covers heads of both (it agrees with one of them only) or where two of their heads have the same number of links; `esverify` does not compare heads of different lengths with each other. Against an administrator who runs the sources as well, the collectors do not help: they can rewrite the collectors' files too. A collector learns only the heads: how fast the whole store grows, not what it holds.
